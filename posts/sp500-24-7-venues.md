This started with the perpetual futures. For most of the last decade a "perp" (a futures contract that never expires, held in line with its underlying by a funding payment) was something you traded on an offshore crypto exchange, on crypto. In 2026 that changed fast, and the S&P 500 came along with it:

- **March:** [trade[XYZ]](https://trade.xyz) listed an S&P 500 perp on Hyperliquid (`xyz:SP500`) as a builder-deployed HIP-3 market, [referencing the licensed S&P 500 index](https://www.buildix.trade/blog/sp500-perpetual-hyperliquid-tradexyz-licensed-24-7-trading-2026) for non-US users.
- **May 29:** the CFTC approved Kalshi's bitcoin perpetual, the [first perpetual listed on a CFTC-regulated US exchange](https://www.proskauer.com/alert/opening-the-door-to-perps-the-cftc-approves-us-listed-perpetual-futures).
- **September 3:** Polymarket launched [perps on its international platform](https://www.blockhead.co/2026/09/07/polymarket-launches-perpetual-futures-with-up-to-20x-leverage-across-crypto-stocks-commodities/), with an S&P 500 contract (now listed as USA500) among the first markets. US users are pointed to Polymarket's separate regulated US venue instead.
- **October 6:** Kalshi launched its [US 500 perpetual](https://coincentral.com/kalshis-us-500-perpetual-future-tracks-500-large-us-companies), a stock-index perp that US residents can trade. It references the MerQube US Large Cap Index rather than the S&P 500 itself, which is close enough to track but worth knowing.
- **November 2-3:** the CFTC let [perpetuals on individual US stocks](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2026/11/03/cftc-clears-perpetual-futures-on-stocks-bringing-crypto-trading-model-to-wall-street) through as well, and CME has [gone to court](https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/equity-perpetuals-and-kpi-contracts-regulatory-approvals-and-legal-challenge) over how these contracts are being approved.

So within a year the S&P 500 went from something you trade on CME (or through SPY) during US hours to something you can trade around the clock on a DEX, a prediction market and a regulated US exchange, in USDC, at leverage most brokers would never offer. The question I actually wanted answered was simple: are these new contracts any good? Do they track the real market, are they fast, and is the weekend price worth anything?

Once I started wiring them up I found they weren't alone. The offshore exchanges had been quietly listing S&P and SPY perps all year (Gate and MEXC in January, Bitget in February, OKX in March, Deribit in September), and tokenized SPY trades in at least five flavors (Backpack's SPYB, which also trades on Binance; Backed's xStocks SPYx; Ondo's SPYon; Robinhood's own SPY token; Bitget's RSPY) on exchanges and in on-chain pools on Solana, BSC, Ethereum and Robinhood Chain. So the tracker grew to all 34 of them. It subscribes to every one at once, converts every quote to E-mini (ES) points, and logs every price change with both the venue's own timestamp and the time it reached me. I left it running from Thursday evening through Monday morning's cash open: about 90 hours, two US cash sessions, a full weekend, and the Sunday 6pm ET reopen. The reference is the CME E-mini itself, 15,983 prints of it.

The short version: the new perps are the best of the bunch. Polymarket's and trade[XYZ]'s contracts lead the whole market by a second or more, and trade[XYZ] tracks CME to about 3 points. The weekend market is real but it doesn't know anything CME doesn't. The leverage is real too, and higher than you'd guess (up to 200x on one contract). And the "free money" gaps between venues mostly turn out to be the price of crossing them.

## The pitch, point by point

### Weekend liquidity

This one is true, with an asterisk the size of the weekend.

CME's E-mini trades Sunday 6pm to Friday 5pm ET. Nearly every venue in this test kept publishing a price through the weekend (the exceptions were two on-chain pools and HTX's SPY perp, which went quiet for long stretches), but publishing a price and trading are different things. Kalshi and the ImpliedOpen aggregator published all weekend and changed price exactly zero times. So the useful measure is price *changes* per minute:

So on this weekend, the weekend market was a place to trade S&P exposure, not a place to find out where the S&P was going. One weekend is an example, not a law; a weekend with real news on Saturday could look different, and that's the obvious thing to capture next.

### Instant liquidity (no T+1 hold)

Sell SPY at a US broker and the cash settles T+1 (the US moved from T+2 to T+1 in May 2024), and moving it out takes bank rails on top of that. Close a perp on any of these venues and the PnL is in your USDC balance immediately; withdrawing it on-chain is a matter of minutes. Tokenized SPY on a DEX settles in the same transaction as the trade.

The honest caveat is the last hop. "Instant to USDC" is not "instant to dollars in a bank": getting off a stablecoin still goes through an exchange or ramp, and that's where identity checks and bank settlement come back.

Those are the caps for the smallest positions. Most venues tier them down as size grows; MEXC's SPY contract, for example, steps from 100x to 50x, 25x, 20x and 10x. For comparison, CME's E-mini is already leveraged: maintenance margin has been [around $25,000](https://www.barchart.com/futures/quotes/ES*0/profile) per contract on roughly $378,000 of notional, so about 15x. Kalshi's US 500 is roughly CME-like; everything offshore goes well past it.

The difference that matters isn't the number, it's how liquidation works. Perps liquidate you automatically and continuously, including at 3am on a Saturday, and that's where the weekend data above stops being academic. The Sunday gap was 36 points, about 0.48% of the index:

| Leverage | Loss on margin from the 36-pt reopen gap | From the 55-pt weekend slide |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 15x (about CME, or Kalshi) | 7% | 11% |
| 50x (trade[XYZ], Polymarket, Bitget, OKX) | 24% | 36% |
| 100x (MEXC SPY, Gate SPX500) | 48% | 73% |
| 200x (MEXC SPX500) | liquidated | liquidated |

That's before fees, funding, or the maintenance margin that gets you liquidated well before you hit 100%. Leverage on a market that gaps when its reference reopens is a different instrument from leverage on a continuous one.

### Faster data than CME (for free)

True, and the gap is big. The free CME feed I used as a reference (TradingView's `CME_MINI:ES1!`) is delayed by exactly 600 seconds; the delay is announced in the feed itself. Real-time CME data needs a paid exchange entitlement. Every crypto venue in this test streams real time over a websocket for free, stamped with the exchange's own clock, and the on-chain venues can be read straight from the chain: the Uniswap pool on Robinhood Chain was a live `eth_subscribe` to its swap events, with the price decoded from the event itself.

A cluster of SPY perps (MEXC, OKX, Bitget, Gate) sits within 1.5 points of CME, and MEXC's three SPX500 contracts sit about 1 point under. Then there are steady offsets: Arcus about -12, Bitget's RSPY token -10, Deribit -10, SPYB on Binance -5. And the tokens on DEXs look all over the place, with Ondo SPYon on Ethereum and BSC at +64 and xStocks SPYx on Solana DEXs at +47, but most of that is bookkeeping, not mispricing. Both tokens reinvest dividends through an on-chain multiplier (about 1.0095 on SPYon and 1.0075 on SPYx, set in the Solana mints' scaled-amount config), so one token is worth slightly more than one share. Venues that quote the raw token sit roughly 0.75-0.95%, or 55-72 ES points, above venues that quote the scaled amount, which is most of the gap.

On top of that there's the 1-3 second lead from the fast perps, and the weekend, where the whole complex sat 36 points away from where CME was about to open. That's the case for "more efficient markets": these venues add price discovery outside CME hours and outside CME's fee structure. The catch is whether any of it is yours.

## What arbitrage actually costs

### Fees

One ES point is about 1.3 bps. An arb has two legs, and you open and close both, so that's four fills. At an illustrative 5 bps taker fee per fill, a round trip costs about 20 bps, or roughly **15 ES points**, which is more than most of the gaps in the chart above. Maker rebates and fee tiers change this a lot, which is exactly why the people doing it at scale are the ones on the lowest tiers. Perps also charge (or pay) funding, which accrues the whole time you hold the position.

This is also why the steady premiums are steady. Arcus sitting 12 points under CME with only ±3.7 points of tracking error isn't a mispricing anyone forgot to trade; a stable offset like that is the market pricing in fees, funding and the cost of moving capital between venues. A gap you can arbitrage is one that moves, not one that sits still.

### Slippage

The quote isn't the fill. Many of these books are thin: several of the DEX rows went a full minute or more without a single price change for over 90% of the capture, and when I investigated one Solana SPYB pool that was disagreeing with the rest, it turned out to trade about $7,000 a day. Its price was simply stale. A gap on a venue like that disappears the moment you try to trade into it, usually against you.

Capital has to be pre-positioned on both sides too. Moving USDC between exchanges takes minutes, which is several lifetimes of a 2-second lead.

### Colocation

The lead/lag numbers make the fast perps look catchable from home: a 2-3 second head start, and I was receiving their updates in a few hundred milliseconds. But the market makers who *create* that lead are colocated or close to the venues' matching engines, see the same move in single-digit milliseconds, and have usually already traded it by the time a home connection hears about it. You aren't competing for a 3-second window; you're competing for the first few milliseconds of it, against people who paid to be there.

### Premium RPC and websocket feeds

The free tier is not built for this. Two things I hit during the capture:

- Jupiter rate-limited the three Solana token feeds 733 times, which forced them from a 3-second poll to a 30-second one. On-chain reads through public RPC endpoints get similar treatment.
- Several exchanges silently drop a websocket that doesn't send the keepalive they expect (MEXC after about a minute, Bitget after about two), and TradingView drops one that answers its heartbeat in the wrong framing. My first version of the tracker reconnected TradingView about three times a minute and the MEXC contracts dozens of times an hour until I fixed it. Each reconnect is a few seconds of blindness, which would be fatal to any strategy that depends on seeing a move first.

Serious operators pay for dedicated RPC nodes, premium data feeds, and the engineering to keep them up. That's a fixed cost you have to earn back before the arb pays anything.

## No KYC?

Two different questions get mixed up under "no KYC": what a venue asks you for, and what actually stops someone it says it doesn't serve. A terms-of-service page that says "not available to US persons" isn't a control. So for each layer, here's what is actually enforced and by what.

[kycnot.me](https://kycnot.me/?categories=exchange), a directory that grades exchanges by how much identity checking they do, gives a useful vocabulary: level 0 is "Guaranteed no KYC" and level 4 is "Mandatory KYC" ("required for key features, and may be required arbitrarily at any time"), with "Rare" and "Shotgun" KYC in between for services that only ask when something gets flagged. It's built around bitcoin and monero, so of the venues here it only lists [Binance](https://kycnot.me/service/binance), at level 4. The rest sort themselves into three layers.

### Custodial exchanges: KYC is the control

| Venue | What's required |
|---|---|
| Binance | "All new users are required to complete [Verified] to access Binance products and service offerings" ([FAQ](https://www.binance.com/en/support/faq/detail/360027287111)) |
| Deribit | "Deribit requires clients to perform personal verification (KYC) before they will have access to deposits" ([deribit.com](https://www.deribit.com/)) |
| Kalshi | "US-based Kalshi users who have completed KYC verification are eligible to apply" ([help](https://help.kalshi.com/en/articles/15357656-applying-for-perpetuals-access)) |
| Bitget | "You will need to comply with our identity verification procedures before opening an Account" ([terms](https://www.bitget.com/terms/legal/360014944032)) |
| Phemex | ID plus a selfie with liveness detection; without it the 24-hour withdrawal limit is 0 USDT ([guide](https://phemex.com/help-center/articles/phemex-identity-verification-kyc-guide)) |
| MEXC | unverified users are told to "complete real-name verification before continuing with withdrawals" ([KYC FAQ](https://www.mexc.com/support/kyc/kyc-faq)) |

These hold your money, so their rules have teeth. A VPN changes your IP address; KYC checks your passport, so an ID or address in a restricted country fails at onboarding wherever your traffic appears to come from, and bank and card deposits carry a country of their own. Phemex and MEXC will take a deposit without ID but won't let it back out. And the enforcement continues after sign-up: MEXC's [user agreement](https://www.mexc.com/terms) says that if "you have given false representations of your location or place of residence," it can act "including termination of any Account immediately and liquidating any open positions." On a custodial exchange the risk isn't getting blocked at the door; it's getting found out later with a leveraged position open and your collateral on their books.

### Wallet-only perps: the website is the control

trade[XYZ], Polymarket's international site and Arcus all start with connecting a wallet. trade[XYZ]'s [terms](https://trade.xyz/terms) say "To use the Interface, you must connect a non-custodial wallet," and Polymarket's [sign-up](https://help.polymarket.com/en/articles/13369877-how-to-sign-up) takes Google, an emailed code or a wallet, with no ID step. The only technical check is an IP block on the website, which a VPN defeats, and underneath the website the chain checks nothing at all: Hyperliquid's order book accepts any signed order from any address. Both trade[XYZ] and Polymarket ban VPNs in writing ("You must not use a VPN, proxy, Tor, or similar to circumvent geo-blocks or our eligibility rules," and Polymarket "strictly prohibits the use of VPNs or similar tools to bypass geographic restrictions"), but on these venues that's a contract term, not a mechanism.

### DEX pools: nothing is the control, until the issuer acts

And you don't need a perp venue at all. The tokenized SPY pools in this test trade straight from a wallet, with no account and no front end required. Jupiter is just an aggregator that routes a swap across Solana pools; the rest are individual pools on Solana, BSC, Ethereum and Robinhood Chain. It's peer-to-pool rather than peer-to-peer: your wallet trades against a smart contract holding both tokens, at a price set by the pool's balance. At the end of the capture they were doing real volume:

| Token | Chain | Pools | 24h volume |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Robinhood's SPY token | Robinhood Chain | 30 | $13.2M |
| xStocks SPYx | Solana | 28 | $2.55M |
| SPYB | BSC | 9 | $691k |
| Ondo SPYon | Ethereum | 5 | $25k |
| Backpack's SPYB | Solana | 5 | $25k |
| Ondo SPYon | BSC | 1 | $156 |

That's about $16.5 million a day of S&P exposure changing hands with no identity check anywhere in the trade. The issuers' US-person restrictions are enforced where the issuer is actually involved, at minting and redemption ([Ondo](https://docs.ondo.finance/ondo-stocks/eligibility), for example, runs KYC and AML checks there), not in the pool.

What the issuers do keep is a kill switch, written into the tokens themselves. I read the token contracts directly:

| Token | What the issuer can do on-chain |
|---|---|
| SPYx (Solana) | freeze any holder's account, pause all transfers, and move or burn tokens from any wallet (a "permanent delegate"); it can also switch on a transfer hook later |
| Backpack's SPYB (Solana) | the same: freeze, pause, permanent delegate, transfer hook available |
| Ondo SPYon (Solana) | freeze and pause, transfer hook available; no permanent delegate |
| Ondo SPYon (Ethereum, BSC) | an upgradeable contract (the issuer can replace its logic), with role-gated mint and burn-from-any-address functions |
| Robinhood's SPY token | an upgradeable contract with a pause switch |
| SPYB (BSC) | an upgradeable contract with role-gated permissions |

None of them was paused when I checked, none of the Solana mints freezes new accounts by default, and none has an active transfer hook, so there's no allowlist in force today. Nothing stops anyone buying. The control is after the fact: an issuer that decides a wallet shouldn't hold its token can freeze it, and for SPYx and SPYB, take the tokens back.

Wallet-only also cuts the other way. Arcus [lost about $2.23 million](https://cryptoticker.io/en/arcus-exploit-perp-dex-sub-account-bug/) to a sub-account bug on November 10, two days before this capture started. Non-custodial means nobody can freeze your account; it also means nobody is standing behind it.

### What a VPN actually changes

On a custodial exchange, nothing that matters: the control is your identity. On a wallet-only perp venue it gets you past the only technical check there is. On a DEX pool there's no check for it to get past. What's left in the last two cases is the terms you agreed to, the issuer's ability to freeze or claw back tokens later, and the money itself: a wallet still needs USDC from somewhere, and the main on-ramps are the KYC'd exchanges above. The no-KYC services kycnot.me tracks (instant swap services, and peer-to-peer markets like Bisq and RoboSats) deal in bitcoin and monero, not S&P exposure, so any route to an index position without KYC ends on-chain.

None of this makes it allowed. Being able to reach a venue and being permitted to use it from where you live are separate questions, and the second one is about the venue's terms and your local law.

## How this was measured

- **Conversion to ES.** Each instrument family (SPY products, index perps, Kalshi, Polymarket, trade[XYZ]) gets one multiplicative ratio to ES, learned from the CME tape at the CME print's own timestamp (the free feed is 600 s delayed, so every comparison looks up each venue's quote at the moment the CME trade happened, not the moment I received it). A venue's premium is how far it sits from its family after that.
- **Speed.** From a log of every price change (about 1.16 million of them over 3.7 days, half of them from trade[XYZ] alone), not periodic snapshots. Each venue's premium is removed, the "market" is the median of the venues that change price at least twice a minute (leaving the venue being measured out), and lag is the shift, in 250 ms steps, that best lines up each venue's five-second moves with the market's. It's measured separately in the Friday session (6.5 hours) and the first hour of Monday's, then averaged by length. Lags with correlation below 0.2 are dropped.
- **Clock.** My PC's clock wandered by more than a second during the capture as Windows slowly corrected it, and I changed networks partway through. Transport times are corrected per tick against a drift curve built from the fastest feeds and pinned to an NTP check, so differences between venues are reliable, but absolute values carry about ±100 ms.
- **Weekend test.** Each venue's level is the median of its quotes in the five minutes before CME's Friday close and the five minutes before the Sunday reopen; CME's gap is its first five minutes of prints against its last five before the close. Measuring each venue against its own Friday level makes the test independent of its premium. There's a 9-hour hole in the capture on Sunday (07:38-16:50 UTC); it doesn't touch the close or reopen windows.

## Takeaways

- **For speed:** Polymarket Perps and trade[XYZ] lead the market by 1.5-3 seconds, react within the same second, and rarely sit flat.
- **For the level:** the SPY perps on OKX, Gate, Bitget and MEXC track CME within 1.5 points; MEXC's SPX500 contracts within 1.
- **For weekends:** trade[XYZ] and Gate's SPY perp are the most active, but on this weekend no venue said anything about where CME would reopen.
- **Avoid for timing:** MEXC is accurate on level and consistently 5-10 seconds late.
- **On arbitrage:** the gaps are real, but most of them are the cost of crossing them. What's left belongs to whoever has the lowest fees, the deepest pre-positioned capital, and the shortest wire.
